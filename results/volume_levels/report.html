Do high-volume futures minutes mark support / resistance?

What the data says

  • For the big turns the idea is about — no. At a 0.3% (≈ 65 pt) and 0.5% (≈ 110 pt) scale, levels from high-volume minutes held as often as levels from random minutes of the same day (≈ 42–44% each), as support and as resistance, in both halves.
  • At a small scale there is a slight stickiness. At 0.15% (≈ 35 pt), the session's single highest-volume minute held 51.5% of the time vs 46.7% for random levels (z ≈ 3), similar in both halves and on both sides. Price pauses a little there — a few tens of points — but that is too small and too close to a coin flip to trade after costs.
  • Bigger spikes are not better: 20×-volume minutes behaved like 8–12× ones.
  • Generally, a re-tested level breaks more often than it holds (≈ 43% held vs ≈ 47% broke at 0.3%), whatever made it — so a single nice-looking example on a chart says little.
  • Practical use: a high-volume minute is fine to mark on the chart as context, not as a level to sell against or fade.

Held % — volume levels vs random levels of the same day

Held = after NIFTY came back to the level, it moved the chosen distance back on the side it came from before moving that far through the level. z = two-proportion test vs the random levels; |z| below ~2 is not reliable.

Method & limits

  • Continuous near-month NIFTY futures volume (Fyers expired-F&O 1-min data + Arrow for listed contracts), NIFTY spot 1-min closes, Oct 2021 → Oct 2026.
  • Volume levels: each session's highest-volume minute ("day-top") and every minute with volume ≥ 8× the median of the previous ~375 minutes ("spike"); 09:15 and the last 5 minutes skipped. Random levels: 5 other minutes of the same session.
  • Return: within 10 sessions NIFTY first moves the chosen distance away, then trades back to the level. Levels never returned to are left out.
